# JSP-000073 · Differentiability of the totient-ratio distribution

[Problem entry](../catalog-0001-0100.md#JSP-000073) · [Problem index](../README.md)

## Answer

Let

$$
F(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}
\#\left\{1\le n\le N:\frac{\varphi(n)}{n}<t\right\},
\qquad 0\le t\le1.
$$

Schoenberg proved that this limit exists and defines a continuous, strictly increasing function, with $F(0)=0$ and $F(1)=1$. Erdős proved that its associated probability measure is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure. Consequently, $F$ is differentiable at Lebesgue-almost every point of $(0,1)$ and

$$
F'(t)=0\qquad\text{for Lebesgue-almost every }t\in(0,1).
$$

These classical results are reviewed in [Tenenbaum–Toulmonde, §1.1](https://doi.org/10.7169/facm/1229442631). Continuity means that replacing $<$ by $\le$ in the definition gives the same function.

There is also a dense set of points where no finite derivative exists. For every positive integer $m$, put $r=\varphi(m)/m$. Then

$$
\lim_{h\downarrow0}\frac{F(r)-F(r-h)}{h}=+\infty.
$$

The proof below establishes this one-sided limit; it does not assert that the two-sided derivative is $+\infty$. At the endpoints, the inward derivatives satisfy $F'_+(0)=0$ and $F'_-(1)=+\infty$.

Every finite derivative is nonnegative because $F$ is increasing. **Whether a finite, strictly positive derivative exists at even one point remains open.** This is the specific question posed by [Erdős in 1995, §9](https://www.ime.usp.br/~yoshi/resenhas/abstracts/Erdos.pdf#page=6), now numbered [Erdős Problem 50](https://www.erdosproblems.com/50). The [problem database record](https://github.com/teorth/erdosproblems/blob/5ca6b58dccc3e7a41d7db5f2cafdb944599edc4c/data/problems.yaml#L812), checked on 17 September 2026, still lists it as open. A full pointwise description of the differentiability set is therefore not known. The almost-everywhere result does not establish that the derivative is zero wherever it exists.

Here, differentiability in $(0,1)$ means existence of a finite, ordinary two-sided derivative. The endpoint statements use one-sided limits within $[0,1]$.

## A local lower bound and its proof

The distribution admits the probabilistic representation

$$
X=\prod_p\left(1-\frac1p\right)^{B_p},
\qquad
\Pr(B_p=1)=\frac1p,
\qquad F(t)=\Pr(X<t),
$$

where the $B_p$ are independent and $p$ runs over the primes. Indeed, the Chinese remainder theorem gives these probabilities for any finite collection of prime divisibility conditions. The logarithmic tail has the uniform bound

$$
\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}
\sum_{\substack{p>y\\p\mid n}}\log\frac p{p-1}
\le\sum_{p>y}\frac1p\log\frac p{p-1}
\le2\sum_{n>y}\frac1{n^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The same bound holds for the expected random tail. Thus finite truncations converge to the same limiting law. The full random logarithmic sum has finite expectation, so the product is positive almost surely.

Fix $0<\alpha\le1$. There is a constant $c_\alpha>0$ such that every squarefree positive integer $m$ with $r=\varphi(m)/m\ge\alpha$ satisfies

$$
F(r)-F(r-h)\ge
\frac{c_\alpha}{\varphi(m)\log(1/h)},
\qquad 0<h<\min(r,1/2). \tag{1}
$$

To prove this, set $y=\lceil8/h\rceil$. Require $B_p=1$ for every $p\mid m$, including those larger than $y$, and $B_p=0$ for every $p\le y$ that does not divide $m$. The probability of this event is

$$
q=\frac{1}{\varphi(m)}
\prod_{p\le y}\left(1-\frac1p\right)
\prod_{\substack{p\mid m\\p>y}}\left(1-\frac1p\right)
\ge\frac{\alpha}{\varphi(m)}
\prod_{p\le y}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
$$

The last inequality follows because the product over the large prime divisors of $m$ is at least $r$. The remaining random sum

$$
T=\sum_{\substack{p>y\\p\nmid m}}B_p\log\frac p{p-1}
$$

is independent of this event and has expectation at most $2/y\le h/4$. Also, $T>0$ almost surely: independence and the divergence of $\sum_{p>y,\,p\nmid m}1/p$ imply that the probability of selecting no remaining prime is zero. Markov's inequality therefore gives $\Pr(0<T<h)\ge3/4$.

On the intersection of these events,

$$
r-h<re^{-T}=X<r,
$$

since $r\le1$ and $e^{-T}>1-T$. It follows that $F(r)-F(r-h)\ge3q/4$. Mertens' product theorem,

$$
\prod_{p\le y}\left(1-\frac1p\right)
\sim\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log y},
$$

where $\gamma$ is Euler's constant, proves (1), with a constant independent of $m$ and $h$.

For fixed $m$, division by $h$ in (1) gives a quantity tending to $+\infty$, because $h\log(1/h)\to0$. A general positive integer has the same totient ratio as the product of its distinct prime divisors, so the conclusion holds for all $m$.

These ratio values are dense in $(0,1)$. To see this, write $a_p=-\log(1-1/p)$. The positive numbers $a_p$ tend to zero and have divergent sum. Starting with sufficiently large primes, a finite sum can cross any prescribed positive number with arbitrarily small overshoot. Exponentiating the negatives of these sums gives totient ratios arbitrarily close to any point of $(0,1)$.

## Endpoint derivatives

At $t=1$, take $m=1$ in (1), using $\varphi(1)=1$. This proves $F'_-(1)=+\infty$. More precisely, [Tenenbaum–Toulmonde, Theorem 1.2](https://doi.org/10.7169/facm/1229442631) gives

$$
1-F(1-h)\sim\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log(1/h)}
\qquad(h\downarrow0).
$$

At $t=0$, independence and monotone convergence give

$$
\mathbb E X^{-2}
=\prod_p\left(1-\frac1p+\frac1p\left(1-\frac1p\right)^{-2}\right)
<\infty.
$$

Each factor is $1+O(p^{-2})$, so the product converges. Hence

$$
0\le\frac{F(h)-F(0)}h
\le h\,\mathbb E X^{-2}\longrightarrow0,
$$

which proves $F'_+(0)=0$.

## A necessary condition at any finite differentiability point

The uniformity in (1) also gives a restriction on approximation by totient ratios. If $x\in(0,1)$ and $F'(x)=L<\infty$, then every sequence of squarefree positive integers $m_j$ satisfying

$$
r_j=\frac{\varphi(m_j)}{m_j}\to x,
\qquad r_j\ne x,
$$

must satisfy

$$
\varphi(m_j)|r_j-x|\log\frac1{|r_j-x|}\longrightarrow\infty. \tag{2}
$$

For a proof, put $d_j=|r_j-x|$ and suppose that, along a subsequence, $\varphi(m_j)d_j\log(1/d_j)\le K$ for some fixed $K>0$. Eventually $r_j\ge\alpha=x/2$. For any fixed $0<\varepsilon<1$, (1) with $h=\varepsilon d_j$ yields

$$
\liminf_{j\to\infty}
\frac{F(r_j)-F(r_j-\varepsilon d_j)}{d_j}
\ge\frac{c_\alpha}{K}>0.
$$

On the other hand, differentiability at $x$ implies

$$
F(r_j)-F(r_j-\varepsilon d_j)
=L\varepsilon d_j+o(d_j).
$$

Choose $\varepsilon$ so small that $L\varepsilon<c_\alpha/K$. This is a contradiction, proving (2).

Condition (2) is necessary for any finite derivative, including zero. It does not decide whether a finite positive derivative exists: that would require an additional argument ruling out such a point or rigorously constructing one.
